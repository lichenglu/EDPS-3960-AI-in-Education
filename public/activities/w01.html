Tomorrow, Eunseo will put up welcome signs at the learning center. Pip helped draft one of the signs. Chenglu asks you to check which instructions visitors should get and test how the AI tutor could help Eunseo check them.

Your job is to make three moves. Ask for useful help as an AI user, check an output as a critic, and change and test a response rule as a developer. All the notices, drafts, and replies you need are on this page.

Allow about 105 minutes of active work. Use private notes or a printed copy. Only your final insight goes in the ungraded Canvas orientation check.

I Do · 30 minutes

Check the workshop notices · 7 minutes

Think about a suggestion your phone makes while you type or a recommendation a video site makes. Pick one you would check before acting on it and say why. For those examples, read this text or watch the opening of the captioned Crash Course video.[1]

  • Organizer’s notice. “First-time visitors should check in at the welcome desk. Volunteers enter through the side door.”
  • Eunseo’s request. “Draft one clear sign for first-time visitors using the organizer’s notice.”
  • Pip’s draft. “First-time visitors: check in at the side door.”

A fresh notice. “Visitors should wait in the lobby. Volunteers should meet in the courtyard.” Eunseo writes on his own, “Visitors: please wait in the courtyard.”

What would you check before posting the signs? Say what the check might tell you and who could be affected. Compare with Chenglu’s note.

Practice checking information · 8 minutes

Digital literacy includes locating and evaluating information.[2] Data literacy includes checking what a label or a record means.[3] AI literacy includes using AI and evaluating what it gives you.[4]

  1. You find an old screenshot and a current notice signed by the organizer. How would you check which version to use?
  2. The welcome list records each person as a “visitor” or a “volunteer.” What do these labels tell you? Which directions apply to each group?
  3. Someone forwards Pip’s sign for visitors with the notice cropped out. How would you find the current notice and check that the direction applies to first-time visitors?

For each job, name a skill it uses. Explain one place the skills overlap. Compare with Yinqi’s note.

Follow Yinqi’s worked example · 5 minutes

The assigned reading describes user, developer, and critic perspectives.[5] The center makes those perspectives visible as three actions. A user states the intended task and supplies context. A critic compares a specific claim with a source and considers who could be affected. A developer changes how the tutor works, then compares the replies on the same input. Changing a tutor rule is the center’s hands-on example of a developer decision.

Yinqi shows all three moves. The organizer’s note reads, “Visitors should pick up a name tag from the lobby table. Volunteers should wear their staff badge.” Yinqi asks, “Draft one clear sign for visitors using the organizer’s note.” Pip writes, “Visitors: wear your staff badge.” Decide which lines of the note you would use to check the sign.

Predict the reply · 5 minutes

Yinqi reruns the same request after changing the response rule from Draft the sign to Ask what the source says first. Predict what Pip will say first and what Yinqi would have to do next.

Compare both runs · 5 minutes

Use Yinqi’s two replies after making your prediction. Describe what stayed the same, which rule changed, and whose work each reply makes visible. Yinqi also reports feeling confident that he can check AI replies. What would you need to see him do to assess that ability on a new notice?

Developer · Run the same test · 9 minutes

Keep the organizer’s notice and Eunseo’s request fixed. Pick a first-response rule and predict who will do the next step. Open the scripted replies. Record what changed and what you would test before using the rule for more signs.

What do the AI literacy checks tell us? · 15 minutes

These fictional responses illustrate items in three instruments. SNAIL asks people to rate their ability to identify weaknesses of AI. Eunseo selects 6 on a 1–7 agreement scale.[6] MAILS asks them to rate their ability to assess the limitations and opportunities of AI use. Eunseo selects 8 on a 0–10 scale.[7] In an adapted scored GLAT item, an AI summary of a study is available. Eunseo chooses to trust it because it reads well.[8]

Explain what a self-rating shows and what one scored answer can show. Then suggest a short task that would let you observe a user, a critic, or a developer action. Compare your response with Chenglu’s note.

Exchange a Drift Bottle · 15 minutes

Write your own observation or test about Pip’s welcome sign before reading a classmate’s note. Post it in the Canvas course-only discussion if the instructor has opened it. Read a note and reply with a specific source, question, or test. Write one sentence in your private notes about what you would keep or change.

Instructor example. “The organizer’s notice says visitors should check in at the welcome desk. I’d keep the same request and test whether the new rule asks Eunseo to find that line before drafting.” If a peer note is unavailable, reply to this example and continue.

You Do · 25 minutes

File an independent recommendation · 15 minutes

Fresh case. The organizer’s notice says, “Workshop attendees should show their confirmation email at the welcome desk. Volunteers should bring their staff badge.” Eunseo asks Pip to check his draft, “Workshop attendees: bring your confirmation email,” against the notice. Pip replied, “Workshop attendees: show your staff badge at the welcome desk.”

Write your own recommendation before opening feedback or reviewing a peer’s response. Write a request for Pip that states the goal, audience, and source. Support your judgment about the reply with a specific line from the organizer’s notice. Choose a response rule and predict its first reply with the same request and notice. Explain what Chenglu could ask Eunseo to do on a new sign before giving any help. Eunseo rates his ability to check AI claims 6 out of 7. On a scored GLAT item, he chooses to verify an AI-generated research summary against the original paper. Explain what each response can tell Chenglu about his AI literacy.[8]

Test the filed rule · 6 minutes

After your first recommendation, save it as a separate version. Read the fixed reply for the rule you chose. Compare it with Pip’s original reply and with your prediction. Keep your original and label any changes as a revision.

Open fixed replies and feedback after you file your first recommendation
  • Draft the sign. Pip repeats, “Workshop attendees: show your staff badge at the welcome desk.”
  • Ask what the source says first. Pip asks, “Which line tells workshop attendees what to show, and where should they go?” Eunseo replies, “The notice says to show their confirmation email at the welcome desk.”

The original reply gives attendees the volunteers’ badge instruction. In the source-first reply, Eunseo identifies the organizer’s instruction. This is evidence of a source check with a prompt. For independent evidence, give him a new notice and collect his check before offering feedback. His rating shows confidence, and the scored response shows one decision on one item.

Guided comparison notes

Use each note after you have made the corresponding prediction or judgment.

Opening choice

The organizer’s notice tells first-time visitors to check in at the welcome desk. A sign that sends them to the side door could keep them from reaching check-in. Eunseo’s new sign shows how he matched a different notice to the intended audience on this attempt.

Three literacy examples

Digital literacy helps you check where a notice came from and when it was issued. Data literacy helps you see how the labels were defined. AI literacy helps you see which part of the generated draft needs verification, using both of those skills.

Yinqi’s demonstration

Under Draft the sign, Pip wrote, “Visitors: wear your staff badge.” Under Ask what the source says first, Pip asked, “What does the note tell visitors to pick up, and where?” Yinqi answered, “A name tag from the lobby table. The badge is for volunteers.” The note and request stayed the same. Yinqi’s answer shows his source check. His self-rating shows how capable he felt.

The guided judgment

The organizer describes separate directions for first-time visitors and volunteers. Pip gave first-time visitors the volunteers’ direction. Cite the organizer’s line and explain how the sign could misdirect a newcomer.

The guided replay

Under Draft the sign, Pip gives a direction for the sign. Under Ask what the source says first, Pip asks, “What does the notice tell first-time visitors to do?” Eunseo replies, “Check in at the welcome desk. The side door is for volunteers.” That shows what Eunseo could point out with a prompt. A fresh notice can show what he checks on his own.

The guided measurement comparison

SNAIL and MAILS collect people’s judgments about their own skills. A scored GLAT response records their choice on a task. For the adapted scenario, the useful check is to cross-check the original paper. A new task where the learner identifies a claim and checks it against a source can show the steps that learner takes.
